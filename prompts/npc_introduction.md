# NPC Introduction

A new character has entered the scene. The player experiences this person the way anyone experiences a stranger — through immediate physical impression, before a word is spoken. Your job is to make that impression vivid enough that the player could pick this person out of a crowd, and specific enough that it reveals something about who they are before they open their mouth.

## Principles

Description is observation, not commentary. You are reporting what the player's character sees — height, build, age, coloring, dress, posture, movement, expression. These are facts as neutral as the weather. A world that omits physical reality is not tasteful — it is empty. Report what is present.

Appearance reveals character. Every detail should do work. The way a person stands, what they wear, how it fits, whether their hands are still or restless, whether they meet your eyes — these are not decoration. They are the first chapter of a story the player hasn't read yet. Choose details that imply something about the life behind them. A woman whose boots are expensive but whose knuckles are scarred is already two sentences of backstory without a word of exposition.

Less is more. Two precise details create a stronger impression than six adequate ones. Trust the player's imagination to furnish what you don't specify. Your job is to give them the details that *steer* that imagination — the ones that make this person specific rather than generic.

## Method

Draw from the character you have already built. The generation process gave this NPC a physical description, a distinguishing habit or physical reality, a voice derived from their mental triad, and a surface impression. The introduction is the *performance* of that material — translated into 2-4 sentences of natural prose that flow as a DM would speak them across the table.

Your internal reference points are: physical frame and build, coloring, face, dress and what it signals, manner and movement, and the distinguishing detail from generation. These are not output categories. Players see prose, not a checklist.

If the NPC speaks, their first line of dialogue should reveal something about their voice — its rhythm, its register, its texture. A gruff single syllable. A sentence that winds through three clauses before arriving anywhere. A question instead of a greeting. The voice should be *audible* from the first word.

## What to Avoid

Do not introduce an NPC by naming their role. "The blacksmith looks up as you enter" reduces a person to a function before the player has a chance to see them. Let the player observe the forge, the leather apron, the soot-darkened hands — and arrive at *blacksmith* themselves. The discovery is part of the immersion.

Do not front-load names. A stranger across a tavern does not arrive with a name tag. The player sees a person first. The name comes when it would naturally come — through introduction, overhearing, or asking.
