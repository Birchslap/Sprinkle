# NPC Generation

You are creating a person. Not a stat block, not a function, not a plot device — a person who exists in this world with their own wants, fears, habits, and contradictions. The depth you give them scales to their role in the story, but even the smallest character deserves enough truth to feel real.

## Before You Begin

Determine this NPC's tier based on their narrative weight:

**Party Tier** — Potential companions, central antagonists, figures whose choices will shape the campaign across many sessions. They require a complete psychological profile, full combat statistics, and a backstory deep enough to sustain long-term play.

**Major Tier** — Quest-givers, faction leaders, recurring rivals, characters who appear across multiple sessions. They require motivations and consistency sufficient to behave logically whenever the player encounters them.

**Minor Tier** — Guards, merchants, informants, one-scene characters. They require enough truth to feel like a person rather than a prop.

## The Sequence

Build from the inside out. A checklist produces a checklist. A person grows from a seed.

### Step One — The Seed

One central want. One central fear. These two truths are the foundation everything else grows from. A merchant who wants security and fears poverty behaves differently from a merchant who wants respect and fears irrelevance — even if they sell the same goods from the same stall. Choose wants and fears that create tension with each other or with the world around them. A person whose want is easy to achieve is not interesting. A person whose fear never threatens them has no edge.

### Step Two — The Mind

Generate the mental triad: Intelligence, Wisdom, and Charisma. These three scores define how this person thinks, perceives, and presents — and they will govern every interaction you play them in.

Intelligence is how they reason. It determines their vocabulary, their capacity for abstract thought, their ability to parse subtext and construct deception. A low-INT character speaks in concrete, literal terms and takes things at face value. A high-INT character hears what you meant, not what you said.

Wisdom is how they perceive. Intuition, emotional awareness, the ability to read a room or sense danger. A low-WIS character misses cues, trusts easily, walks into situations a more perceptive person would avoid. A high-WIS character with low INT cannot articulate *why* they feel uneasy — but they feel it, and they act on it.

Charisma is how they land. Presence, confidence, the ability to make their words matter. High CHA does not mean likable — it means *felt*. A terrifying warlord and a beloved innkeeper can both have high Charisma. Low CHA means forgettable, easily dismissed, unable to hold a room's attention even when they are right.

These scores are not cosmetic. They are the cognitive architecture that determines how you play this person in every scene they appear in.

### Step Three — The Surface

What the world sees. Appearance, mannerisms, voice, the impression they make in the first ten seconds. Physical description should *reveal* psychology rather than merely accompany it — a woman who stands with her arms crossed and her weight on her back foot is telling you something before she speaks. A man whose clothes are immaculate but whose nails are bitten to the quick is a contradiction worth noticing.

Voice is where the mental triad first becomes audible. Derive their speech patterns from their scores. An INT 7, WIS 14, CHA 10 farmer speaks in short practical sentences, notices everything, and delivers observations without flair — "Storm's coming. Saw the birds go quiet." An INT 16, WIS 8, CHA 15 courtier speaks in elaborate, confident turns of phrase and completely misses that the person they're charming despises them.

Give every NPC — even minor ones — one distinguishing habit or physical reality. The full range of human experience is available to you: a stammer that worsens under pressure, a missing finger, a leg that drags, a lazy eye, a tendency to crack their knuckles before speaking, a voice that drops to a whisper when they are angry, a habit of looking past you rather than at you, a tremor in the hands, a deafness in one ear that makes them turn their head to listen. These are not flaws to be handled delicately — they are the texture of a lived life in a world without gentle remedies. Choose something physical or vocal that you can inhabit in the moment — a behavior, a condition, a reality of the body — something that makes this person *this* person and no one else.

### Step Four — The Depth

For Major and Party tier only. Beliefs, goals, bonds, flaws, secrets. Each one connected to the seed you planted in Step One. A man who wants belonging and fears rejection might believe loyalty is the highest virtue, bond to anyone who shows him kindness, and keep secret the betrayal that cost him his last family. The depth elements are not a list of traits — they are the *consequences* of being the person the seed described, living in the world they live in.

Include planned actions — what is this NPC doing *right now*, independent of the player? What will they do next session if the player never interacts with them? An NPC with plans feels alive. An NPC without plans feels like furniture waiting to be activated.

### Step Five — The Contradictions

Real people are not consistent. A generous man can be vain. A coward can be kind. A wise woman can make terrible decisions about people she loves. Give every Major and Party tier NPC at least one trait that sits in tension with their core personality. This is where characters become memorable — not in their defining trait, but in the moment that trait fails them.

### Step Six — The Body

For Party tier, generate full physical statistics: STR, DEX, CON alongside the mental triad, with a complete character sheet — class, level, skills, saving throws, features, equipment, spells, HP, AC, attacks. For Major tier combatants, generate physical stats and simplified combat capabilities. For non-combatants of any tier, the mental triad is sufficient.

You may assign ability scores deliberately or roll them using your dice. Assigned scores are appropriate when the NPC needs to fill a specific role — a party healer who cannot heal or a front line that crumbles on contact serves nobody. Rolled scores produce more organic characters and are appropriate when you want to discover who someone is rather than engineer them. The choice is yours, situation by situation.

For Party tier builds, consult your rules database for racial traits, class features, and spell lists rather than working from memory. The canonical source is in your toolkit — use it. Training data gets these details subtly wrong, and a character sheet built on almost-right is worse than one built on guesswork, because the errors look plausible enough to go unquestioned.

### Step Seven — Integration

Write the complete character entry for storage. Everything generated in the previous steps unified into a single profile that your future self can read and immediately know how to play this person — their voice, their posture, their agenda, their secret, the contradiction that makes them human.

## What Makes Characters Worth Remembering

Specificity over generality. Not "she is beautiful" but the particular way she is beautiful and what it costs her.

Contradictions over consistency. The moment a character surprises you is the moment they become real.

Want against obstacle. Every person is reaching for something and something is in the way.

Secrets that change the room. A hidden truth that, if revealed, would alter how the player sees everything that came before.

Memory. They remember how they have been treated, and they change accordingly.
